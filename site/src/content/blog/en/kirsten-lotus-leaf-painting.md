The first thing you notice is not the paint. It is the leaf. A real lotus leaf,
the size of a dinner plate or bigger, with fine veins running out from the
centre like the spokes of a wheel. It has been dried, pressed and stretched on a
wooden frame, and it looks delicate and surprisingly sturdy at the same time.

That is what you paint on with Kirsten. Her workshops go by the name Himapan
Berlin, which sounds like somewhere far away. That is the point.

## What lotus leaf painting is

In lotus leaf painting the leaf itself is the canvas. The leaves are harvested
in Thailand and preserved so that they can be stretched on a frame without
tearing. Then you paint on them, and the paint settles into the texture of the
leaf. The veins stay visible, so every picture carries a pattern you could never
plan.

In Bangkok the technique is taught in workshops under the very name Kirsten
uses: Himapan. Thailand is not the only place where leaves become pictures.
In Vietnam, too, craftspeople make pictures from lotus and bodhi leaves,
preserving them with limewater, sunlight and a lot of patience. Leaves as a
surface for art are not an oddity in Southeast Asia; it is an idea that has
grown up in more than one place.

## Why the lotus

Few plants carry as much meaning in Asia as the lotus. In Thailand you find it
everywhere: in temples, in painting, in sculpture, in ceremonies.

The meaning comes from Buddhism. The lotus is rooted in mud, grows up through
murky water and opens its flower above it, clean and untouched. In one of the
old discourses the Buddha compares himself to such a flower, rising from the
water and unstained by it. Ever since, the lotus has stood for purity, for
clarity, for waking up in the middle of a messy world. The Vietnamese monk Thich
Nhat Hanh put it in four words: *no mud, no lotus.*

Nobody painting on a lotus leaf thinks about this all the time. But it is
there, somewhere between the veins.

## The name: Himapan, the forest at the foot of the world mountain

Himapan, often spelled Himmapan, is a place that does not exist. In Thai
Buddhist cosmology it is a legendary forest at the foot of Mount Meru, the
mountain at the centre of the universe, described in the *Traiphum*, an old Thai
text about the three worlds. The Himapan forest is home to creatures that are
half one thing and half another: nagas, the serpents of the water; garudas with
the heads of birds; kinnaris, half woman and half bird.

You still see them on Thai temples and palaces today. A forest full of
imagination and colour: a fitting name for a kind of painting where you never
quite know what will end up on the leaf.

## How Kirsten found it

Kirsten has always painted, much of it in acrylic. About three years ago she
took part in a lotus leaf painting workshop run by a woman who had lived in
Asia for a long time and was looking for someone to take over her workshops.

Kirsten thought it would be a shame to let such a rare craft simply disappear.
So she decided to carry it on, alongside everything else she does. She could
not take over the atelier itself: it was in the south of Berlin and she lives in
the north. So she turned the idea around. Instead of a fixed room, she now
brings everything with her and comes to the group.

Practically nobody offers this kind of painting in Germany. Apart from Kirsten
there is one other person, in the Harz mountains, who learned from the same
teacher. It is rare in the rest of Europe, too.

## The material: a work of art before the first brushstroke

The lotus leaves are harvested fresh in Thailand. Then comes a process of about
three weeks whose exact recipe is a secret. Most likely a solution similar to
glycerine is involved, so the leaves stay supple and do not break. After that
they are dried and pressed.

They are mounted on hand-made wooden frames from an atelier in Bangkok. You
cannot buy these frames in Germany; they come by ship. Kirsten tried making
them herself. It did not work. "The frame alone is a work of art," she says.

## How you paint

If you are picturing watercolour or canvas, prepare to be surprised. You do not
just paint on the leaf, you work the paint into it: with a sponge, with your
fingers, layer by layer. It is slow, and that is exactly the appeal. You sink
into it. Kirsten sees it again and again: people who arrive restless go quiet
after a while.

No talent needed. With her guidance everyone goes home with a beautiful
picture. The smallest format, 40 × 40 cm, takes three to four hours. Larger
formats can take several days.

And not everyone has to paint their own. At an event in the Gärten der Welt,
thirty to forty visitors worked on one single picture, one pair of hands after
another. For a team that is a lovely idea: one shared picture that ends up on
the office wall.
